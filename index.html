September 14th: Art Academies and Hierarchy of Genres

The Academicians of the Royal Academy
Artist:
Johann Zoffany
Made:
1771-72
Place:
Royal Academy, London

Depiction of a life drawing class. Note how Angelica Kauffmann and Mary Moser are depicted as paintings on the wall rather than physically present (which was considered inappropriate and therefore not permitted).

Connection: Barring woman from attending life drawing classes (where académies were made) limited their ability to study anatomy and therefore their ability to become history painters.

(Source)
Académie

"A sketch of the human body made after a live model; the basis of art education in the eighteenth century" - Study Guide

Context: A nude figure drawing or painting, typically made from a live model. It was a fundamental part of academic art training, which progressed from copying classical art, to drawing plaster casts, and finally to studying live models.

Connection: The Academicians of the Royal Academy portrays a scene where they would be making Académies

Source
Écorché

"A preserved, flayed body that reveals the underlying musculature of the human body; these were typically made from the bodies of executed criminals" -Study Guide

(Source)
Reception Piece

"A work submitted by an artist to an academy for approval as part of the requirements for admission to membership" - Study Guide

Example: The Embarkation for Cythera

(Source)
Hierarchy of Genres

"Established by the Académie royale, which ranks genres according to their complexity and moral function" -Study Guide

Higher genres were valued for their intellectual depth rather than the mechanical imitation of physical objects.

(Source)
History Painting

"the most complex and elevated genre, said to incorporate all the lower genres. Large-scale, usually based on narratives from classical history or from the Bible. Intended to provide moral instruction and articulate shared civic values. An attempt to highlight painting as a intellectual endeavor, rather than a manual craft form." -Study Guide

(Source)
Portraiture

Grand manner portraiture:
"A subgenre of portraiture that attempts to elevate it by incorporating classical allusions." -Study Guide
(Source)

Conversation piece:
"A subgenre of portraiture specific to the eighteenth century; it is a form of group portraiture that depicts a relatively informal group of family members or friends" -Study Guide
(Source)

Genre Painting

"Depicts scenes of everyday life; these scenes can have moral significance through symbolism (bubbles referring to the fleeting nature of life, etc.)" -Study Guide
(Source)

Fête galante:
"A subgenre specific to the eighteenth century; it depicts aristocratic figures in masquerade costumes relaxing and playing in lush natural settings. They are typically amorous in nature" -Study Guide

Basically an elevated (or higher worth) version of genre painting. Somewhat ambiguous position in the hierarchy of genres.
(Source)

Landscape painting

The second lowest genre due to the absence of the human figure or a narrative.

(Source)
Still Life

The lowest ranked genre, dismissed as a mechanical labor of copying an inanimate object by sight.

(Source)

Rocaille [French for "rockwork"]

"Term used to describe a type of European ornament of variable form, resembling the irregular edges of rocks and shells. By the 1730s it became an important element of the Rococo style." -Study Guide

(Source)
Grotto

An artificial recess or structure made to resemble a natural cave.

(Source)
Boiserie

"Profusely decorated wall paneling that covers the wall up to the ceiling and may also be painted or gilded." -Study Guide

Modern Moral Subjects

"The modern moral subject is a type of painting that was invented by English artist William Hogarth (1697-1764), which satirizes the manners and morals of the period in which he lived." -Study Guide

(Source)
St. Martin's Lane Academy

"Originally operated between 1720 and 1724, it was reopened by William Hogarth in 1735. It became the center of artistic training in London until it was subsumed with the founding of the Royal Academy in 1768. Unlike the Royal Academy, it was run on democratic club principles rather than rigid academic hierarchies." -Study Guide

(Source)
The Analysis of Beauty (1753)

"Written by William Hogarth, this treatise attempts to offer a theoretical underpinning for the Rococo style." -Study Guide

It's ironic how he disliked the French aristocracy and their shallow morals, but his book provided the logical, scientific justification for the Rococo style (S-curves, line of beauty).

Denis Diderot

"Denis Diderot (1713–1784): French philosopher and art critic who rejected the Rococo and argued for a Neoclassical style." -Study Guide

(Source)
Herculaneium and Pompeii

"Two ancient Roman towns destroyed by the eruption of Mount Vesuvius in 79 CE. Their excavation in the mid-eighteenth century informed Neoclassical design, especially in interior decoration." -Study Guide

(Source)
Ara Pacis

"An altar dedicated to the Pax Romana—the Roman peace—instigated by Augustus’s rule. Benjamin West borrowed from its design in his creation of Agrippina Landing at Brundisium with the Ashes of Germanicus." -Study Guide

(Source)
Johann Joachim Winckelmann (1717-1768)

"Classicist and author, he was a key figure in the rise of Neoclassicism. His climate theory of art and human variation informed the associations between whiteness and Neoclassicism that exist to this day. He viewed the Greeks as the apogee of human perfection, and assumed non-Greek (and non-Western) cultures were degraded and incapable of such perfection." -Study Guide

(Source)
Polychromy

"The practice of decorating sculptures and architecture with different colors." -Study Guide

Maison Carrée de Nîmes

Cincinnatus (519-430 BCE)

"A Roman general who was summoned out of retirement to aid Roman forces in battle. Aso soon as the battle was won, he relinquished power and to private life on his farm. Houdon makes clear reference to Cincinnatus in his sculpture of Washington." -Study Guide
